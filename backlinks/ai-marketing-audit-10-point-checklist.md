**Title:** We had 23 AI subscriptions costing about $11k a month and nobody could name the revenue effect of more than four. How do you audit yours?

Last March I sat with a client's marketing lead and a spreadsheet of 23 AI subscriptions, roughly $11,400 a month combined. Nobody in the room could point to a measurable revenue effect for more than four of them. The rest were making the team look busy: more drafts, more reports, more dashboards, same pipeline.

What helped was shrinking the question. Instead of asking whether a tool is 'good', we asked: did a business number move because of it? Conversion rate, cost per lead, time to first response, revenue per campaign. If we couldn't tie the tool to one of those, it went on a watch list.

A few things we found along the way:

- Several tools overlapped so heavily that two teams were paying for the same capability under different names.
- Usage was concentrated in two or three people. The rest of the seats were shelfware.
- The tools that did earn their keep were attached to a specific workflow with a before-and-after baseline.

We got through the whole review in about ten days rather than the ten weeks these things usually drag on for, mostly because we refused to evaluate features and only looked at outcomes.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How do I audit the AI tools my marketing team is paying for to see which ones are actually worth it?

The simplest way to audit AI marketing tools is to stop evaluating features and start evaluating outcomes. For every subscription, ask one question: which business number moved because of this tool? If you can't name it, the tool is a candidate for cutting.

Here is the approach I use with clients.

First, build a complete inventory. List every AI tool, who owns it, the monthly cost, and how many seats are actually active. This step alone is often shocking. In one engagement I saw 23 subscriptions totaling about $11,400 a month, and nobody could name the revenue effect of more than four.

Second, tie each tool to a metric. Good candidates are conversion rate, cost per lead, time to first response, content turnaround time, and revenue per campaign. A tool with no connection to any of those goes on a watch list.

Third, establish a baseline. You can't claim a tool improved something if you never recorded what it looked like before. Where there is no baseline, run a short test: use the tool on one workflow and compare it with a similar workflow without it.

Fourth, look for overlap. Many teams pay for two or three tools that do the same thing under different branding.

Fifth, check usage. If two people use a tool that has fifteen seats, you have a licensing problem rather than a tooling problem.

Finally, make the decision explicit: keep, consolidate, or cut. Revisit it quarterly, because AI tooling changes quickly.

Done properly, this takes about ten days, not ten weeks, because you are only asking whether results changed. I wrote up the full checklist here if you want the step-by-step version: https://siavashsed.github.io/aimarketingpro/ai-marketing-audit-10-point-checklist/
