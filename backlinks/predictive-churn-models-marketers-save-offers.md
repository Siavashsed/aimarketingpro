**Title:** Is your churn model the problem, or is it what marketing does with the score?

I audit churn setups for subscription brands, and I keep seeing the same thing. The model works fine. What marketing does with the warning doesn't.

One case stuck with me. A meal-kit account's dashboard had flagged 11,200 customers as high risk. The week before, every one of them had already been sent a 30% discount. Same offer, same timing, no matter why each person was at risk.

That causes two problems. First, a lot of customers have already made up their minds by the time a churn score crosses the high-risk line. The useful signal comes earlier, when engagement starts slipping, not after it's gone. Second, a blanket discount teaches your best customers to wait for one. It also does nothing for people who are leaving because of delivery problems or because they're bored of the menu.

Here's what has worked better for me. Set the trigger earlier. Group flagged customers by the likely reason they're leaving. Then match the offer to that reason, like skipping a week, switching plans or fixing the service issue, before you reach for price.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How should marketers act on the risk scores from a predictive churn model?

I'm a performance marketing director, and I audit retention programs for subscription brands. The honest answer is that most teams already have a good enough model. Where they go wrong is in what they do with the score.

Three things matter most.

1. Timing beats accuracy. A churn score only helps if it fires while the customer can still be persuaded. Many teams set the high-risk threshold so conservatively that by the time someone gets flagged, they have already decided to leave. I would rather act on a moderately confident signal two weeks early than on a near-certain one two days before cancellation. Watch the early signs: fewer logins, skipped orders, ignored emails, a smaller basket.

2. Know why each customer is at risk. A risk score tells you who might leave, not why. Split flagged customers by the likely reason: price sensitivity, product fatigue, service problems or a change in their situation. Most modern models can show which factors drove a score, so use that.

3. Match the offer to the reason. This is where the money gets wasted. I once worked with a meal-kit brand whose dashboard flagged 11,200 customers as high risk. The week before, every one of them had been sent the same 30% discount. People who were bored of the menu didn't need money off; they needed variety. People with delivery problems needed the problem fixed. And loyal customers learned to expect a discount. Offers like pausing a subscription, downgrading the plan or getting a service fix usually cost less than a discount and keep more customers.

Finally, measure against a holdout group. If you don't hold back some flagged customers who get no offer, you can't tell whether a save offer kept them or whether they would have stayed anyway.
